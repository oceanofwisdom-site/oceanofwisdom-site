Indian Philosophical Traditions: Knowledge, Self and Liberation

An introduction to the diversity of Indian philosophical debate, with careful distinctions among Vedic, Buddhist, Jain and later systematic schools.

1. A landscape of debate, not one doctrine

Indian philosophy is not a single worldview. It comprises traditions that disagree about what exists, how knowledge is justified, whether a permanent self exists, what causes suffering and what liberation means. The familiar division between āstika and nāstika schools concerns their relation to Vedic authority; it should not be mistaken for a simple division between theism and atheism.

2. Knowledge and justification

Classical Indian philosophers developed detailed theories of pramāṇa, or reliable means of knowledge. Schools debated the status of perception, inference, comparison, testimony and other proposed sources. These were not merely abstract lists. Their acceptance or rejection shaped arguments about the self, the external world, language, causation and religious knowledge.

The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy’s entry on epistemology in classical Indian philosophy describes a long-running tradition of debate across distinct systems. This makes it useful to approach Indian philosophy through specific problems and arguments rather than through broad labels such as “Eastern spirituality”.

3. Selfhood: contrasting arguments

Some Upanishadic and Vedānta traditions investigate the relation between ātman and ultimate reality, though Vedānta itself contains important disagreements. Advaita Vedānta, associated with Śaṅkara, argues for non-duality; Viśiṣṭādvaita and Dvaita develop different accounts of the relation between self and Brahman. These schools should not be collapsed into one position.

Buddhist philosophical traditions develop analyses of impermanence, dependent arising and non-self. The doctrine of anātman challenges the idea of an independent, enduring essence. Later Buddhist schools—including Madhyamaka and Yogācāra—develop sophisticated arguments about language, cognition and the nature of phenomena. Jain philosophy, by contrast, affirms individual living selves and connects liberation with the removal of karmic bondage, disciplined conduct and non-violence.

4. Ethics, action and liberation

Philosophical systems link knowledge to ways of living. Ethical disciplines, attention, debate, ritual, meditation and renunciation may all have roles, but their status differs by school. Jain accounts place exceptional emphasis on ahiṃsā and careful restraint; Buddhist traditions connect ethical conduct and insight with the cessation of suffering; Vedānta schools debate knowledge, devotion and action in relation to liberation.

5. How to compare traditions

Comparison should begin by reconstructing each tradition on its own terms. Ask what problem a philosopher addresses, what assumptions are made, which arguments are offered and how rival schools respond. Translate technical terms carefully and identify the historical period: a doctrine formulated in a later scholastic debate should not be projected unchanged into the earliest Vedic period.

The result is a richer picture than a claim that all traditions teach the same thing. Shared questions can coexist with real disagreement. Respectful comparison does not erase difference; it makes the intellectual achievement of each tradition visible.
