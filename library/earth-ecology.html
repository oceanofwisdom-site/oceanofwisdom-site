Water, Landscape and Ecological Knowledge in South Asia

A source-led study of water harvesting, settlement ecology and climate-responsive design, with attention to regional variation.

1. Water systems belong to landscapes

Water infrastructure is never only a structure. Its performance depends on rainfall, catchment area, geology, slope, soil, vegetation, settlement density, maintenance and rules of access. Tanks, wells, stepwells, reservoirs, canals and household storage systems developed in different combinations because regions faced different hydrological conditions.

It is therefore inaccurate to speak of one uniform “ancient Indian water system”. The subcontinent contains monsoon-fed river basins, arid zones, highlands, coastal plains and seasonal streams. A method effective in one landscape may be unsuitable in another.

2. Harappan water management

Archaeological evidence from Harappan settlements shows that water was an important part of urban planning, although practices varied by site. Dholavira in present-day Gujarat is especially instructive. UNESCO’s site documentation describes reservoirs, channels and water-harvesting arrangements adapted to an arid island supplied by seasonal streams. The city’s built environment, craft production and regional trade must be considered together; its water structures were part of a wider urban system.

Archaeological remains can establish the presence, form and spatial relationships of structures. They cannot by themselves reveal every rule governing access, labour or ownership. Those questions require careful interpretation and, where available, comparison with inscriptions and later historical records.

3. Tanks, stepwells and community maintenance

Across different periods, communities built and maintained tanks, ponds, stepwells, wells and channels for domestic use, irrigation and other purposes. Some systems depended on local institutions and collective labour; others were sponsored by rulers, merchants, religious institutions or wealthy donors. Their social histories are as important as their engineering.

Research on Jaipur’s traditional water systems, for example, documents the relationship between stepwells, kunds, tankas, lakes and later piped networks. Such case studies show how urbanisation and centralised supply can lead to the neglect of local infrastructure. They also caution against romanticising the past: systems could fail when catchments were built over, maintenance weakened or access became unequal.

4. Climate-responsive architecture

Traditional architecture offers a repertoire of responses to heat and sunlight: shaded verandas, courtyards, screened openings, thick walls, high ceilings, orientation and ventilation paths. These techniques are not universally interchangeable. Their effectiveness depends on climate, humidity, wind, occupancy and materials. Modern building science can test them through thermal modelling, measurement and post-occupancy evaluation.

5. Applying historical knowledge responsibly

Historical systems should be studied as evidence, not copied as ready-made solutions. Contemporary adaptation requires water-quality safeguards, public-health standards, equitable access, catchment protection, maintenance funding and updated rainfall data. Restoring a pond while allowing its feeder channels to be blocked will not restore its hydrological function.

The central lesson is systems thinking. Water security depends on the relationship between land, infrastructure, institutions and people. Historical study helps identify long-term practices and design principles; modern science and local participation determine whether they can work today.
