How to Research History: Evidence, Sources and Scholarly Method

A practical scholarly guide to primary sources, provenance, corroboration, translation, citation and responsible interpretation.

1. Begin with a question, not a conclusion

Reliable research begins by defining a question that can be investigated. A broad topic such as “ancient wisdom” may be meaningful as a theme, but it is too large to verify as a single claim. A researchable question identifies a period, place, community, text, object or process. For example: what do surviving inscriptions reveal about land grants in a particular region and century?

Separate the question from the answer you hope to find. This reduces confirmation bias—the tendency to notice evidence that supports an existing belief while overlooking evidence that challenges it. A strong article makes its method visible and remains open to revision.

2. Distinguish primary and secondary sources

Primary sources are materials produced in or close to the period under study: inscriptions, manuscripts, letters, administrative records, archaeological objects, photographs, oral testimonies or datasets. They are not automatically truthful or self-explanatory. A royal inscription may praise its patron; a travel account may reflect an outsider’s assumptions; an artefact may have been moved from its original context.

Secondary sources are scholarly analyses that interpret primary evidence. These include peer-reviewed articles, academic books, critical editions and specialist catalogues. A useful research process reads secondary scholarship to understand the debate, then returns to the primary evidence to see what it actually supports.

3. Verify provenance, date and context

Provenance concerns the origin and documented history of an object or source. For a manuscript, ask where it is held, how it was catalogued, what its material and script indicate, and whether its chain of custody is known. For an inscription, consider its findspot, material, palaeography, language and archaeological setting. For a web page, identify the author, institution, publication date and editorial process.

Do not confuse the date of a surviving copy with the date of the composition it transmits. Nor should a date inferred from style be presented as an exact date. State whether a chronology is securely dated, approximate or disputed.

4. Evaluate authority and corroboration

Authority is not established by a prestigious-sounding title, a large number of followers or repetition across websites. Look for identifiable authors, relevant expertise, transparent citations, editorial standards and engagement with other scholarship. Peer review is a valuable quality-control process, but it is not a guarantee that every conclusion is correct.

Corroboration means checking a claim against independent evidence. A textual statement may be compared with inscriptions, archaeological findings, environmental data or other contemporary records. Several websites repeating the same unsourced claim do not constitute several independent sources.

5. Handle translation, quotation and uncertainty carefully

Translation is an interpretive act. Technical and culturally specific terms may not have exact equivalents in modern languages. Quote the original where useful, identify the translator and edition, and explain important choices. Avoid presenting a modern paraphrase as a verbatim ancient quotation.

Use quotation marks only for words checked against the cited edition. Paraphrase in your own words and cite the source. If scholars disagree, describe the main positions fairly and identify the evidence each uses. If evidence is missing, say so. “Not yet known” is a legitimate scholarly conclusion.

6. Build a transparent bibliography

Every substantial factual claim should be traceable to a source. Prefer original editions, academic publishers, peer-reviewed journals, university research projects, recognised archives and public heritage institutions. Record author, title, publisher or journal, year, page range and a stable link or DOI. For online sources, note the access date when the content may change.

Keep research notes that distinguish direct quotations, paraphrases and your own interpretation. This makes it easier to avoid accidental plagiarism and to correct an article when new evidence appears. A bibliography should allow readers to follow the trail, not merely decorate the end of a page.

7. An editorial standard for Ocean of Wisdom

Articles in this library should distinguish documented evidence, scholarly interpretation, traditional belief and contemporary speculation. Historical and religious traditions deserve careful treatment, but respect does not require treating every claim as empirically established. Likewise, scientific explanations should be represented according to current evidence, not retrofitted to validate an ancient text.

When evidence is contested, explain the disagreement. When a source cannot be verified, do not cite it as authority. Correct errors transparently. The aim is not to win an argument for a civilisation or worldview; it is to help readers understand how knowledge is established, challenged and refined.
