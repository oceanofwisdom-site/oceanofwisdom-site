Texts & manuscripts

How to read an ancient text

An ancient text is not simply a window into the past. It is a work shaped by language, genre, audience, memory and the circumstances of its preservation. A hymn, legal inscription, epic poem and philosophical dialogue each make different kinds of claims and must be read differently.

Begin by asking who composed or commissioned it, when and where it took shape, which manuscript or inscription survives, and whether the text has passed through layers of editing or translation. Compare reliable editions and translations, distinguish the words of the source from later commentary, and look for independent archaeological or documentary evidence. Where dates or authorship remain disputed, state that uncertainty rather than forcing a single answer.

Inscriptions: public records carved in stone and metal

Inscriptions are among the most valuable sources for reconstructing political, religious and social history. They may record grants, victories, donations, boundaries, construction, memorials or official proclamations. Their apparent directness can be misleading: an inscription is usually a purposeful public statement, not a neutral report of everything that happened.

Researchers examine script, language, palaeography, formulae, material, findspot and later reuse. A royal eulogy may exaggerate a ruler's achievements; a land grant may preserve precise administrative details while omitting the experience of ordinary cultivators. Reading an inscription alongside archaeology and other records helps separate the event being commemorated from the claims made about it.

Astronomy & science

Indian mathematical astronomy: observation and calculation

South Asian astronomers developed sophisticated procedures for calendrical calculation, predicting celestial positions and describing planetary motion. Works associated with Aryabhata, Varahamihira and later astronomers belong to a changing intellectual history, not one unaltered system.

These texts combine mathematical models, parameters, tables and observational traditions. Their historical importance lies in how scholars formulated problems, refined calculations and transmitted methods. A responsible comparison with modern astronomy separates historical achievement from present-day scientific accuracy: an old computational model can be intellectually remarkable without being a current physical description of the heavens.

Calendars and the practical science of timekeeping

Calendars organise recurring natural cycles for civic, agricultural and ritual purposes. Solar years, lunar months and the visible cycles of planets do not fit together as simple whole numbers, so calendar-makers developed intercalation rules and computational schemes. Different societies chose different conventions depending on their priorities and observational traditions.

Historical calendars should be studied in their own terms. A date may be expressed in a regional era, regnal year or lunisolar system; converting it into a modern date requires explicit assumptions. Good historical work identifies the calendar convention and explains uncertainty rather than presenting every conversion as exact.

Earth & ecology

Traditional water harvesting: tanks, stepwells and community systems

Across the Indian subcontinent, communities developed diverse ways to collect, store and distribute seasonal water. Tanks, stepwells, village ponds, channels, wells and rooftop collection systems responded to local rainfall, geology, settlement patterns and agricultural needs.

These systems were not interchangeable, nor were they always sustainable in every period. Their performance depended on maintenance, access rules, catchment protection and collective institutions. Studying them today means examining local engineering and social organisation together, then adapting useful principles—such as slowing runoff, replenishing groundwater and maintaining shared infrastructure—to contemporary conditions.

Passive cooling: lessons from climate-responsive buildings

Buildings can reduce heat gain by responding intelligently to sun, wind, humidity and local materials. Shading, orientation, courtyards, ventilated roofs, thermal mass and controlled openings are among the strategies used in different climates. Their effectiveness depends on local conditions: a technique suited to a dry region may perform poorly in a hot, humid coastal environment.

Traditional buildings are useful case studies, not proof that older construction was always comfortable or sustainable. Modern design can test these principles with building physics, energy modelling and health standards. The strongest approach combines climate knowledge, occupant needs, durable materials and efficient mechanical systems where needed.

Civilizations & history

What archaeology can—and cannot—tell us

Archaeology reconstructs past lives through material evidence: buildings, tools, pottery, food remains, burials, inscriptions and the layers in which objects are found. Context is crucial. An object without a secure findspot or date may tell us far less than a modest artefact recovered from a carefully recorded excavation.

Material evidence can reveal technologies, trade, diet, settlement patterns and environmental change. It rarely provides direct access to every belief, language or political event. Researchers therefore compare archaeological findings with texts, scientific analyses and regional evidence, while keeping competing interpretations visible. The absence of evidence is not automatically evidence of absence.

Trade routes and cultural exchange across the Indian Ocean

The Indian Ocean connected East Africa, Arabia, South Asia and Southeast Asia through seasonal sailing patterns and port networks. Merchants moved textiles, spices, metals, ceramics and other goods, while sailors, migrants, scholars and religious communities carried languages, technologies and ideas.

These networks were not a single route controlled by one power. Their shape changed with monsoon knowledge, political conditions, port facilities and commercial demand. Archaeological finds, shipwrecks, inscriptions and travel accounts each illuminate different parts of the story. Exchange created connections, but it also involved competition, unequal power and local adaptation.

Philosophy & traditions

Selfhood and liberation in Indian philosophy

Indian philosophical traditions ask whether the self is permanent, changing, relational or ultimately misunderstood. Upanishadic schools, Buddhist thinkers and Jain philosophers developed distinct answers, using careful argument as well as contemplative and ethical disciplines.

Some Vedanta traditions identify the deepest self with ultimate reality; Buddhist analyses challenge the idea of an independent, enduring self; Jain thought describes living beings as individual conscious substances. These positions should not be collapsed into a single “Eastern view.” Their differences are precisely what make comparison fruitful: each offers a distinctive account of knowledge, responsibility and freedom.

Ahimsa and ethical responsibility

Ahimsa, commonly translated as non-violence or non-harming, has a significant place in several Indian traditions, especially Jain, Buddhist and Hindu ethical thought. Its meaning and practical demands vary. Some traditions place strong emphasis on disciplined restraint in relation to all living beings; others connect non-harming with intention, duty, compassion and the circumstances of action.

Rather than treating ahimsa as a slogan, study how thinkers connect it to truthfulness, self-control, livelihood, food, political power and responsibility. Ethical ideals become clearer when we examine both their philosophical arguments and the difficult choices involved in applying them.

Arts & culture

Temple architecture as a record of society

Temples are religious spaces, but they are also historical records. Their plans, construction techniques, sculpture, inscriptions and patronage reveal changing relationships among artisans, rulers, donors, ritual specialists and local communities.

Architecture varies by region and period. A careful study identifies the building's date, materials, later additions and original setting before assigning it to a style or dynasty. Inscriptions and archaeological context can illuminate how a monument was used and maintained. This approach lets us appreciate artistic achievement while avoiding the assumption that every temple expresses one uniform belief or political purpose.

How to interpret a work of art responsibly

Start with the object itself: material, scale, technique, condition, inscriptions and visible features. Then investigate provenance, date, place of production, patronage and original setting. A work's current museum label may be useful, but it may not capture every scholarly debate or the object's full history of movement and ownership.

Iconography should be interpreted with regional and historical context. Similar motifs can carry different meanings in different communities, and an artwork may have served ritual, political, domestic or commercial purposes. Careful interpretation combines close looking with reliable catalogues, archaeological evidence and respect for the communities connected to the object.
