Reading a sacred landscape: place, route and community

Sacred geography studies how religious and cultural communities understand, organise and move through space. A mountain may be revered as a divine presence; a river may be a source of purification and livelihood; a shrine may become the centre of a regional pilgrimage network. A monastery, temple complex or historic city may connect teaching, ritual, patronage, trade and everyday life.

Three ideas should be kept distinct. A sacred site is a place invested with religious significance. A pilgrimage route is a journey connecting places and communities. A cultural landscape is a wider environment shaped over time by human activity and cultural meaning. These categories overlap, but they are not interchangeable. Archaeology can document buildings, inscriptions, objects and settlement patterns; it cannot by itself decide what a place means to a worshipper today.

Routes are shaped by rivers, passes, coastlines, seasonal conditions and settlement networks. Pilgrimage can transform a town temporarily through festivals, processions, markets, hospitality and the movement of offerings. Research therefore considers not only permanent monuments but also gathering spaces, approaches, water sources and the relationship between built sites and their surroundings.

Water, ecology and sacred space

River confluences, tanks, wells, springs and temple reservoirs may support daily life as well as ritual practice. Their physical histories include changing channels, construction phases, repairs, sedimentation and new patterns of use. The same water body can have ecological, economic and religious significance without those meanings being reducible to one another. Research on southern Indian waterscapes shows how contemporary landscapes retain traces of past water infrastructure and land use. This is a reminder that sacred and inhabited places are evolving environments, not monuments frozen in time.

For each site, ask what can be established from archaeology and dated records, what comes from later textual traditions, and what is maintained through living community practice. A later pilgrimage text may preserve valuable religious memory, but it cannot automatically establish the date of an earlier building.

Case study: Hemakuta Hill and the layered landscape of Hampi

Research on the Hemakuta hill area at Hampi examines the persistence and transformation of Śaiva sacred associations between approximately 800 and 1325 CE. The study cautions against treating a pilgrimage landscape as homogeneous. Different shrines, narratives, patrons and communities can give neighbouring places distinct histories, even when later visitors experience them as one sacred zone. This layered approach is useful far beyond Hampi: a sacred landscape is often a record of successive generations rather than the product of one founding moment.

How to investigate a sacred place responsibly

  • Use archaeological layers, building phases and inscriptions to establish chronology.
  • Read texts in their language, genre and historical setting; do not treat all traditions as contemporaneous.
  • Include oral histories and living practice while identifying them as community memory and current meaning.
  • Consider environmental history: water availability, erosion, flooding, land use and urban growth.
  • Ask whose landscape is being described—patrons, monastic institutions, local residents, artisans, traders or pilgrims.

A strong atlas combines these perspectives and makes uncertainty visible. It does not present one tradition as the only history of a region.
