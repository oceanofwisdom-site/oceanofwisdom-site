Buddhism — Gautama Buddha and Buddhist Masters

Origins and historical setting

Buddhism began in northern India around the fifth century BCE. Siddhartha Gautama, the Buddha or Awakened One, taught a path to the ending of suffering.

Central teachings

The Four Noble Truths explain suffering, its causes, its cessation and the path to cessation. The Noble Eightfold Path joins ethics, mental cultivation and wisdom.

Texts, practices and communities

Theravada, Mahayana and Vajrayana developed in different historical settings. Influential teachers include Nagarjuna, Vasubandhu, Buddhaghosa, Bodhidharma, Dogen and Padmasambhava.

Legacy and continuing life

Buddhist communities spread across Asia. Monasteries, universities, art, medicine, debate and translation played major roles. Contemporary Buddhism includes monastic and lay communities.

The Buddha’s teaching in practice

The early discourses present the Four Noble Truths as a practical diagnosis: dukkha describes the instability and unsatisfactoriness of conditioned existence; craving and ignorance sustain it; cessation is possible; and the path trains conduct, attention and understanding. The eight factors are commonly grouped as wisdom, ethical conduct and mental cultivation.

The Buddha’s first sermon is traditionally associated with the Deer Park at Sarnath. His teaching community included monks, nuns and lay followers. The early Buddhist councils are remembered in Buddhist traditions, although details of their dates and proceedings remain subjects of historical discussion.

From Ashoka to the great centres of learning

In the third century BCE, Emperor Ashoka’s inscriptions record his support for the dhamma and the Buddhist community, while also urging respect among religious groups. Buddhist missions and networks helped carry teachings beyond the subcontinent.

Nalanda, in present-day Bihar, developed into a major monastic and intellectual centre from the early centuries CE, flourishing especially from about the fifth century. It attracted students and teachers from across Asia. Its history belongs to a wider network of monasteries and centres, not to a single uninterrupted institution.

Later Buddhist philosophical traditions explored questions of dependent arising, emptiness, consciousness and the nature of knowledge. Nagarjuna is associated with Madhyamaka; Vasubandhu with influential Yogacara and Abhidharma works. These schools debated one another and shaped Buddhist thought across Asia.
