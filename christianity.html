Christianity — Jesus Christ

Origins and historical setting

Christianity began in first-century Judea within a Jewish religious setting. Jesus of Nazareth taught in Galilee and Judea; his followers proclaimed his resurrection and formed communities across the Mediterranean.

Central teachings

The teachings attributed to Jesus emphasise love of God and neighbour, mercy, forgiveness, humility, care for the poor and reconciliation. The Sermon on the Mount and parables remain central to Christian reflection.

Texts, practices and communities

The Bible contains the Old Testament and the New Testament, including the Gospels and writings of early Christian communities. Catholic, Eastern Orthodox and Protestant traditions developed distinctive histories and practices.

Legacy and continuing life

Christian communities shaped art, music, education, hospitals, charitable institutions and philosophy. Their histories also include conflict, colonial entanglements and reform; careful study recognises complexity and diversity.

First-century context and early communities

Christianity emerged within the diverse world of Second Temple Judaism under Roman rule. Jesus and his earliest followers were Jewish; their language, scriptures, festivals and debates belonged to that setting. The movement spread through communities around the eastern Mediterranean, where questions about scripture, worship, identity and the inclusion of non-Jews shaped its development.

Historical study distinguishes what can be established about the first-century setting from Christian theological claims about Jesus. The resurrection is central to Christian faith; historians can study the earliest testimonies, the development of belief and the communities that preserved those accounts without claiming that historical method can adjudicate every theological question.

New Testament texts and their formation

The New Testament contains four canonical Gospels, Acts, letters and Revelation. The Gospels are ancient narratives written in the first century and early second century, drawing on traditions about Jesus and the communities that transmitted them. Paul's letters are among the earliest surviving Christian writings. The canon took shape through a long process of reading, debate, liturgy and recognition across different communities; the process was not identical everywhere at every moment.

Translation matters. The New Testament was written in Koine Greek, while the Christian Bible also includes texts inherited from Jewish scriptural traditions in Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek. English translations reflect decisions about manuscripts, vocabulary and style. Readers should compare translations and consult notes rather than assume that one modern wording is the only possible rendering.

Churches, theology and lived practice

Christianity is internally diverse. Catholic, Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Protestant and other churches have distinct histories, liturgies, structures and theological emphases. Their differences involve authority, sacraments, church organisation, interpretation of scripture and the relationship between faith and reason. Even within these families there are many local traditions and contemporary movements.

Christian intellectual history includes early debates about the nature of Christ and the Trinity, monastic and mystical traditions, medieval scholastic philosophy, Reformation controversies, modern biblical scholarship and liberation theologies. A short overview should not present one church's account as the entire history of Christianity.

Historical legacy and critical study

Christian communities contributed to art, music, education, hospitals, charitable institutions and philosophy. Their histories also include coercion, antisemitism, imperial and colonial entanglements, reform and resistance. A balanced account studies both institutional achievements and harms, and recognises that Christian communities have been shaped by local cultures across Africa, Asia, Europe, the Americas and Oceania.
