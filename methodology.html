A transparent account of how we select, describe and present knowledge — and where uncertainty must remain visible.

Research Methodology

1. Source selection

Where possible, we begin with primary sources: original texts, inscriptions, archival records, official documents, datasets and contemporary testimony. Secondary sources are used to establish historical context, compare interpretations and identify scholarly debates. Institutional repositories, university publications and open-access journals are preferred when reliable material is freely readable.

2. Source hierarchy and attribution

  • Primary source: the historical text, artefact, record or dataset itself.
  • Scholarly source: peer-reviewed research, academic books or specialist reference works.
  • Institutional source: museums, archives, universities and public research bodies.
  • Community or tradition source: material that documents how a living tradition understands and practises its teachings.

References should identify the specific work, passage, edition or record whenever that information is available. A general search-results page is not a substitute for a direct source link.

3. Evidence, interpretation and belief

We aim to label claims according to their status: established evidence, a scholarly interpretation, a tradition's own teaching, a disputed claim or an unverified hypothesis. Religious or philosophical statements are presented as teachings of the relevant tradition, not automatically as empirically established facts. When scholars disagree, the disagreement should be acknowledged rather than concealed.

4. Translation and transliteration

Original scripts are retained where practical and legible. Transliteration should follow a stated or recognisable convention and preserve meaningful distinctions such as vowel length, aspiration and diacritics where applicable. Translations are treated as interpretations, not word-for-word equivalents; significant ambiguity should be explained. Modern translations and editions may be protected by copyright even when the underlying ancient work is not.

5. Cultural and comparative care

Similar ideas across traditions are compared carefully, without assuming that they are identical or that one tradition borrowed from another without evidence. Living communities are described respectfully, and no single interpretation is presented as universally accepted when diversity exists within the tradition.

6. Open access and rights

We prefer freely readable primary sources and research. Open access does not always mean unrestricted reuse. Images, translations, recordings and substantial quotations require item-specific rights checks and attribution. See the Editorial, Sources & Rights Policy.

7. Corrections and review

Readers can report a broken reference, mistranslation, factual error or rights concern through the Contact & Corrections page. Corrections are reviewed against identifiable sources; uncertainty is not removed merely to make a page sound definitive.

Important limitation

This methodology describes the standard we aim to apply. It does not mean every existing page or reference has already passed a full scholarly, translation or rights audit.
