Jainism — Vardhamana Mahavira

Origins and historical setting

Jainism is an ancient Indian śramaṇa tradition. It recognises twenty-four Tirthankaras. Mahavira, the twenty-fourth, renewed and systematised a path already present in the tradition.

Central teachings

Ahimsa, non-violence, is central to Jain ethics. Truthfulness, non-stealing, restraint and non-possession are also important. Anekantavada recognises the many-sided nature of reality and encourages intellectual humility.

Texts, practices and communities

Jain philosophy describes jiva, the soul, as an individual conscious reality. Liberation comes through right faith, right knowledge and right conduct. Śvetāmbara and Digambara traditions preserve distinct lineages.

Legacy and continuing life

Jain communities contributed to Indian languages, logic, literature, architecture and philanthropy. Their teachings on restraint and non-violence remain relevant to animal welfare and ecological responsibility.

Ideas that distinguish Jain philosophy

Jain thought explains liberation through the relationship between jiva (living soul) and ajiva (non-living reality). Karma is understood as a subtle material bondage that adheres to the soul; ethical discipline, restraint and insight help stop new karmic influx and shed accumulated karma.

The Three Jewels—right faith (samyag-darshana), right knowledge (samyag-jnana) and right conduct (samyag-charitra)—form the practical framework of the path. The five great vows are non-violence, truthfulness, non-stealing, celibacy or chastity, and non-possession; their degree of observance differs between mendicants and lay followers.

Anekantavada teaches that complex reality cannot be exhausted by a single standpoint. Syadvada expresses judgements conditionally, reminding the speaker to state the standpoint and limits of a claim. This is not the idea that every opinion is equally true; it is a disciplined account of partial perspectives.

Teachers, scriptures and living lineages

Jain tradition remembers Parshvanatha as the twenty-third Tirthankara, preceding Mahavira. The dates of early Tirthankaras belong to religious tradition and are not all independently verifiable by historians. Mahavira is generally placed in the sixth or fifth century BCE, with traditional dates differing from modern scholarly chronologies.

The Tattvartha Sutra, attributed to Umasvati or Umasvami and composed in the early centuries CE, is an influential systematic work respected across Śvetāmbara and Digambara communities. The two major traditions differ on scripture, monastic discipline and the status of women’s liberation, while sharing core commitments to the Tirthankaras and the path of purification.

The Jain greeting Micchami Dukkadam, especially associated with the forgiveness observances of Paryushana and Das Lakshana, expresses a continuing ethic of accountability and reconciliation.
