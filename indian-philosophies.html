Charvaka / Lokayata

An Indian materialist and sceptical current whose surviving doctrines are known mainly through later accounts by philosophical opponents. Charvaka sources are fragmentary; the school is commonly associated with prioritising perception as a means of knowledge and challenging ritual claims, afterlife doctrines and unseen karmic mechanisms. Traditional attribution to Brihaspati is legendary rather than securely documented. A careful study distinguishes the school's reported positions from caricatures in polemical sources.

Sikhism

A distinct tradition founded in the Punjab through the teachings of Guru Nanak and the Sikh Gurus, centred on devotion, equality and service.

What does āstika and nāstika mean?

Classical Indian doxographies often group schools according to whether they recognise the authority of the Veda. In this context, āstika and nāstika do not simply mean “theist” and “atheist” in the modern sense. The distinction concerns scriptural authority and intellectual affiliation. Buddhist, Jain and materialist thinkers could reject Vedic authority while developing rigorous philosophical arguments of their own.

The familiar six āstika darśanas are Nyāya, Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya, Yoga, Pūrvamīmāṃsā and Vedānta. This is a useful conventional map, but it should not be mistaken for a fixed list that captures every Indian intellectual movement or its chronology. Schools developed over time, interacted and disagreed internally.

Six classical darśanas: different questions and methods

  • Nyāya: logic, inference, debate and reliable sources of knowledge.
  • Vaiśeṣika: categories of reality, qualities, causation and theories of material constituents.
  • Sāṃkhya: an analysis of consciousness and nature, commonly expressed through puruṣa and prakṛti.
  • Yoga: disciplined practice, mental processes and liberation, with philosophical links to Sāṃkhya.
  • Pūrvamīmāṃsā: interpretation of Vedic injunctions, ritual action, language and the authority of scripture.
  • Vedānta: interpretation of the Upaniṣads, Brahma Sūtras and Bhagavad Gītā, with diverse positions on Brahman, self and liberation.

These short descriptions are starting points. Each school contains multiple authors, commentarial lineages and debates; later Vedānta, for example, is not one uniform doctrine.

Śramaṇa traditions and materialist criticism

Buddhist and Jain philosophies developed substantial accounts of knowledge, ethics, causation, selfhood and liberation. Buddhist traditions debate impermanence, dependent origination, no-self and the nature of consciousness. Jain thinkers developed accounts of jīva, karma, non-violence and many-sidedness (anekāntavāda). Neither tradition should be reduced to a reaction against Vedic thought; both have extensive independent textual and institutional histories.

Lokāyata or Cārvāka is known largely through reports preserved by later opponents. It is commonly associated with materialist criticism and a strong emphasis on perception, but the surviving evidence is fragmentary. Claims about its complete doctrine or a single founder should therefore be made cautiously.

Knowledge, argument and pramāṇa

A major strength of classical Indian philosophy is its sustained debate about how knowledge is gained and justified. The technical term pramāṇa refers to a reliable means or source of knowledge. Different schools accept and explain perception, inference, comparison, testimony and other sources in different ways. Nyāya develops detailed accounts of inference and debate; Buddhist logicians analyse perception and inference; Mīmāṃsā and Vedānta debate testimony and scriptural authority. These are not merely abstract disputes: they shape each school's views about reality, language, ethics and liberation.

Texts, languages and historical method

Many classical philosophical works survive in Sanskrit, but Indian intellectual history also includes Pali, Prakrit, Tamil and other languages. A philosophical tradition is not identical with one language, one region or one religious community. Texts are transmitted through manuscripts, commentaries, oral teaching, debate and later editions. Dating a school is often harder than dating a particular surviving text: a text may preserve older ideas, and a later commentator may describe an earlier teacher through the concerns of a new period.

For this reason, this site separates traditional attribution from secure historical dating, and identifies where a position is reconstructed from later sources.
