A comparative history · Evidence-led chronology

World Civilisations & the Journey of Knowledge

Explore how societies developed, exchanged ideas and transformed the human story — across regions and through time.

History did not unfold in one place or along a single line. While cities and states rose in one region, other communities were developing distinct systems of farming, trade, philosophy, science, art and governance. This timeline places selected milestones alongside one another to encourage comparison, not competition.

Showing all milestones
c. 10,000–8,000 BCEGlobal

Farming communities expand in several regions

In parts of Southwest Asia, communities increasingly cultivated plants and managed animals. Independent or partly independent transitions to food production also occurred elsewhere, including China, New Guinea, Africa and the Americas, at different times and under different conditions.

A long transition, not a single invention or universal timetable.
From c. 7000 BCESouth Asia

Mehrgarh and early settled life

Mehrgarh, in present-day Pakistan, provides important archaeological evidence for early farming, herding, craft production and changing settlement patterns in the northwestern subcontinent.

Dates and phases are archaeological estimates that depend on excavation and dating evidence.
c. 3500–3000 BCEWest Asia

Urban centres and early writing in Mesopotamia

Large urban communities developed in southern Mesopotamia. Administrative record-keeping contributed to the emergence of proto-cuneiform and, later, a writing system used for administration, literature and scholarship.

The earliest writing developed in stages; it was not initially identical to modern alphabetic writing.
c. 3100 BCEAfrica

Early dynastic Egypt

Political consolidation along the Nile marks the conventional beginning of Egypt’s Early Dynastic period. Hieroglyphic writing, royal institutions and monumental traditions developed over time within a much older Nile Valley cultural landscape.

The chronology of early rulers and the process of unification remain subjects of specialist study.
c. 2600–1900 BCESouth Asia

Mature Harappan urban civilisation

Major settlements including Harappa, Mohenjo-daro and Dholavira show planned streets, craft specialisation, standardised weights and extensive water-management features. The script remains undeciphered, so claims about language, rulers and religious institutions require caution.

The dates describe the mature urban phase; earlier and later regional phases extend beyond it.
c. 1600–1046 BCEEast Asia

Shang dynasty China

Archaeological sites, bronze vessels and oracle-bone inscriptions provide evidence for Shang political and ritual life. The inscriptions are among the earliest substantial bodies of writing known from China.

The dates are conventional historical estimates; early Chinese chronology contains debated points.
18th century BCEWest Asia

Hammurabi and Babylonian statecraft

Hammurabi ruled Babylon in the eighteenth century BCE. The law collection associated with his name is a royal monument that illuminates legal ideals, social distinctions and kingship; it should not be mistaken for a modern, comprehensive legal code applied uniformly in every case.

The text is a primary source for royal ideology as well as legal history.
2nd–1st millennium BCESouth Asia

Vedic traditions and oral transmission

Vedic compositions were transmitted through highly disciplined oral practices. Their dating, layers and relationship to changing social worlds are studied through language, textual analysis and comparative evidence; no single date captures the formation of the entire corpus.

Textual chronology is complex and differs from the date of surviving manuscripts.
1st millennium BCE onward · chronology debatedSouth Asia

Ramayana traditions and the growth of an epic

The Ramayana is a major South Asian epic with a complex history of composition, oral performance, textual transmission and regional retellings. The Sanskrit text commonly associated with Valmiki contains layers that developed over time; scholars do not treat it as a contemporary chronicle with one securely established date. Its many versions across South and Southeast Asia show how stories, ethical ideals and literary forms travelled and were reinterpreted.

Literary and cultural significance is well documented; the epic’s narrative events and proposed historical dates require separate evidence and should not be presented as settled chronology.
1st millennium BCEMediterranean

Alphabetic scripts and Mediterranean exchange

Alphabetic writing traditions spread and adapted around the Mediterranean. Greek writing adopted and modified a Phoenician consonantal script, including the use of signs for vowels, illustrating how writing systems change through contact.

Cultural transmission is usually adaptation, not simple one-way copying.
c. 6th–5th centuries BCESouth Asia

New philosophical and religious movements in South Asia

In the context of expanding towns and mahājanapadas, śramaṇa traditions including Buddhism and Jainism developed alongside diverse Brahmanical schools and other currents of debate. Their teachings stimulated long-running discussions about ethics, knowledge, suffering, non-violence and liberation.

The dates of teachers’ lives and early textual layers are debated; traditions preserve different chronologies.
c. 550–330 BCEWest Asia

Achaemenid Persian Empire

The Achaemenid dynasty created a vast imperial system linking regions from the eastern Mediterranean to parts of Central and South Asia. Roads, administrative practices, taxation and local arrangements helped govern a diverse empire.

Imperial rule varied by region and changed across successive rulers.
c. 322–185 BCESouth Asia

Mauryan Empire and Ashokan inscriptions

The Mauryan state became one of the largest empires in South Asian history. Ashoka’s inscriptions, distributed across the subcontinent and beyond, provide unusually direct evidence for royal communication, ethical policy and imperial presence.

The inscriptions are primary evidence, but their language and local contexts need careful interpretation.
221 BCEEast Asia

Qin unification of China

The Qin state unified competing kingdoms and established an imperial structure. Measures, scripts and administrative practices were standardised in important ways, although the Qin dynasty itself was short-lived.

Unification was a political process, not the disappearance of regional differences.
1st millennium BCE–1st millennium CEGlobal

Overland and maritime networks connect distant societies

Trade routes across Central Asia and the Indian Ocean linked merchants, ports, caravan towns and courts. Goods moved alongside artistic techniques, religious ideas, languages and technologies. These networks changed over time and were never a single continuous road.

Exchange involved cooperation, competition, migration and unequal power.
c. 4th–6th centuries CESouth Asia

Gupta-period political and intellectual cultures

Several influential works in Sanskrit literature, mathematics and astronomy belong to this broad period, alongside regional developments beyond Gupta political control. The label “Golden Age” is debated because cultural achievement coexisted with social hierarchy and regional variation.

Cultural history should not be reduced to the boundaries or fortunes of one dynasty.
c. 1st–7th centuries CEAfrica

Aksum and Red Sea connections

The Aksumite kingdom in the Horn of Africa participated in Red Sea and wider commercial networks. Its inscriptions, coinage, monumental architecture and adoption of Christianity provide evidence for a powerful African polity connected to neighbouring regions.

Aksum’s history is part of African and Indian Ocean history, not a footnote to Mediterranean events.
618–907 CEEast Asia

Tang dynasty China

The Tang era saw major developments in urban culture, poetry, administration and international exchange. Buddhist institutions and networks connected China with Central Asia and South Asia, while printing technologies developed further in East Asia.

The Tang world was cosmopolitan in important centres, while also marked by conflict and political change.
From 8th century CEWest Asia

Abbasid-era scholarly networks

Under Abbasid rule and in wider intellectual networks, scholars translated, criticised and extended works in philosophy, medicine, mathematics and astronomy. Knowledge moved through Arabic, Persian, Syriac, Greek and other languages, with contributions from people of varied backgrounds.

The so-called translation movement was diverse and extended beyond one institution or city.
c. 250–900 CEAmericas

Classic Maya cities and knowledge systems

Maya city-states developed sophisticated writing, calendrical and astronomical practices, monumental architecture and regional political networks in Mesoamerica. Maya communities continued beyond the Classic period and remain living cultures today.

The “collapse” of some southern lowland centres did not mean the disappearance of Maya people.
1206–1526 CESouth Asia

Delhi Sultanate and changing regional worlds

The Delhi Sultanate comprised several dynasties and shifting political territories. Persianate court cultures, regional languages, architecture, trade and religious institutions developed through interaction and contestation across the subcontinent.

South Asia’s medieval history also includes powerful regional kingdoms and societies beyond Delhi’s reach.
From 15th century CEGlobal

Printing transforms the circulation of knowledge

Woodblock printing had a long history in East Asia; movable type was developed in East Asia centuries before Gutenberg’s European press. Printing in Europe expanded rapidly from the mid-fifteenth century, reshaping the scale and speed of textual reproduction in that region.

Printing history is plural: technologies emerged in different contexts and did not follow one universal path.
1526–1857 CESouth Asia

Mughal Empire and regional transformations

The Mughal Empire reshaped political institutions, revenue systems, architecture, painting and courtly culture across much of northern India at different moments. Regional states, communities and cultural traditions remained active participants in this changing landscape.

The empire’s reach, authority and administrative practices varied considerably over time.
From late 18th century CEGlobal

Industrialisation and a new energy regime

Industrialisation began in Britain and spread unevenly to other regions. Fossil-fuel energy, mechanised production, railways and new forms of finance transformed work, cities, environments and global power relations.

Industrialisation was neither simultaneous nor socially uniform; its environmental costs remain central to modern history.
1945 onwardGlobal

Decolonisation and new international institutions

After the Second World War, anti-colonial movements and political negotiations transformed the international order. India became independent in 1947; many other countries achieved independence across subsequent decades. The United Nations, founded in 1945, became a central forum for international diplomacy.

Decolonisation was a varied process involving mass movements, negotiations, conflict and continuing struggles over sovereignty.

How to read this timeline

Dates marked “c.” are approximate. Broad ranges indicate long processes rather than one-day events. Archaeological periods, textual chronologies and dynastic dates are not always directly comparable. The entries are selected milestones, not a complete list of every society or achievement.

“Civilisation” is used here as a practical historical label for complex and changing societies; it does not imply that some peoples were more advanced or more valuable than others.
