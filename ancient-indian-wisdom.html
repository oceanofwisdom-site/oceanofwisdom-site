Texts, ideas and knowledge traditions of the Indian subcontinent — studied through language, history, philosophy and evidence.

Vedas and Vedic literature

The Four Vedas

Rigveda: hymns and poetic speculation. Yajurveda: ritual formulae and liturgical arrangement. Samaveda: chant traditions. Atharvaveda: hymns, household rites, healing and royal ritual. Each Veda has distinct recensions and layers.

The Vedas were transmitted through highly disciplined oral traditions. Their composition and redaction span long periods; precise dating remains debated.

Brahmanas and Aranyakas

Brahmana texts explain ritual procedures, symbolism and interpretation. Aranyakas form varied transitional bodies of reflection, often connecting ritual practice with contemplation.

These are distinct genres within the wider Vedic corpus, not interchangeable names for the Vedas.

Upanishads and Vedanta

The principal Upanishads

The Upanishads examine self, ultimate reality, knowledge, consciousness, death and liberation. Frequently studied texts include Isha, Kena, Katha, Prashna, Mundaka, Mandukya, Taittiriya, Aitareya, Chandogya and Brihadaranyaka.

They are diverse compositions rather than one uniform philosophical treatise.

Vedanta schools

Vedanta draws especially on the Upanishads, Brahma Sutras and Bhagavad Gita. Advaita, Vishishtadvaita and Dvaita interpret Brahman, self, world, knowledge, devotion and liberation in distinct ways.

Epics and the Bhagavad Gita

Ramayana traditions

The Sanskrit Ramayana associated with Valmiki inspired retellings and performances across South Asia. Kamba Ramayanam, Ramcharitmanas, Adhyatma Ramayanam and regional versions have their own literary settings and theological emphases.

Mahabharata and Bhagavad Gita

The Mahabharata is a layered Sanskrit epic containing narrative, law, political reflection, ethical debate and religious teaching. The Bhagavad Gita is its celebrated dialogue on action, knowledge, devotion and disciplined insight.

Puranic literature

Bhagavata, Vishnu, Shiva and other Puranas bring together mythology, cosmology, genealogy, pilgrimage, ritual and theology. Textual histories and recensions vary.

Classical Sanskrit, Tamil and regional literature

Tamil Sangam literature

The Ettuthokai and Pattuppāṭṭu preserve poetry on love, war, kingship, landscape and generosity. Akam and puram conventions are central to classical Tamil poetics.

Tirukkural

Attributed to Tiruvalluvar, this ethical Tamil classic is organised around virtue, wealth and love. Its couplets have a long interpretive and translation history.

Tamil language and literature resources →

Tolkāppiyam

An early Tamil grammatical and poetics treatise traditionally associated with Tolkappiyar. Its date, layers and relationship to the Sangam corpus remain scholarly questions.

Kālidāsa

Meghadūta, Raghuvaṃśa, Kumārasambhava and Abhijñānaśākuntalam are influential works of Sanskrit poetry and drama traditionally attributed to Kālidāsa.

Buddhist and Jain literature

Indian traditions include Pali canonical collections and Jataka narratives, Sanskrit and Prakrit Buddhist works, Jain Agamas and extensive later commentarial and narrative literature.

Adi Shankaracharya: works and attribution

Upadeśasāhasrī

A major independent work associated with Shankara, presenting Advaita through prose and verse instruction and examining the Self, knowledge and liberation.

Attribution: widely accepted as Shankara's independent treatise.

Brahma Sutra and Bhagavad Gita Bhāṣyas

Commentaries central to Shankara's interpretation of Vedanta and the relationship between knowledge, action and liberation.

Attribution: core works in the Shankara corpus.

Principal Upanishad Bhāṣyas

Commentaries that form a central part of his Vedanta project. Individual texts and recensions still require bibliographic care.

Vivekachūḍāmaṇi, Ātma Bodha and Tattva Bodha

Popular Advaita teaching texts traditionally attributed to Shankara. Their authorship is not equally secure and is questioned in modern scholarship.

Kanakadhara Stotram

Kanakadhara means “stream of gold”. This hymn praises Mahalakshmi and seeks her auspicious glance. A traditional story connects it with Shankara's compassion for a poor woman who offered him an āmalaka fruit; the miraculous shower of golden fruit belongs to devotional hagiography, not independently verified history.

Authorship note: The hymn is conventionally attributed to Adi Shankaracharya and appears in collections of works bearing his name. Its authorship is not established to the same degree as the principal Vedanta commentaries; verse counts and readings vary between recensions.
