Islam — Prophet Muhammad

Origins and historical setting

Islam emerged in seventh-century Arabia. Muslims regard Muhammad as the final prophet in a line that includes Abraham, Moses and Jesus. The Qur’an is understood as revelation from God received by Muhammad.

Central teachings

Tawhid, the oneness of God, is central. Islam emphasises worship, mercy, justice, honesty, charity and responsibility toward neighbours and those in need. The Five Pillars shape Muslim religious life.

Texts, practices and communities

The Qur’an is Islam’s foundational scripture. The Sunnah and hadith literature also inform belief and practice. Sunni and Shia communities, and schools within them, have developed diverse legal, theological and spiritual traditions.

Legacy and continuing life

Islamic civilisation contributed to mathematics, medicine, astronomy, geography, architecture and philosophy through exchanges among scholars and cultures. Its history includes many regions and varied Muslim communities.

Seventh-century Arabia and the emergence of Islam

Islam emerged in western Arabia in the seventh century CE. The Qur'an presents Muhammad as a messenger within a line of prophets that includes Abraham, Moses and Jesus. Early Muslim communities developed in a world of Arabian tribal networks, trade, Jewish and Christian communities and wider Near Eastern political change. The chronology of the earliest events is studied through the Qur'an, later biographical and historical writings, inscriptions, coins and material evidence.

Muslims understand the Qur'an as revelation from God. Historical scholarship examines the formation, transmission and early reception of the text; it should not confuse that historical analysis with the devotional meaning the scripture holds for believers.

The Qur'an, Sunnah and interpretation

The Qur'an is Islam's foundational scripture in Arabic. The Sunnah refers to the prophetic example, while hadith reports transmit accounts used in religious, legal and ethical reasoning. Hadith collections were compiled and assessed through developed scholarly methods; different communities and scholars have not always agreed about the authenticity or interpretation of particular reports.

Translations of the Qur'an are interpretations of its meaning, not replacements for the Arabic text in Muslim worship. A responsible reader compares translations, checks the translator and edition, and recognises the role of commentary (tafsir), language and historical context.

Religious practice and intellectual diversity

The Five Pillars are a widely used summary of central practices: profession of faith, prayer, almsgiving, fasting during Ramadan and pilgrimage to Mecca for those able. Muslim life also includes many forms of scholarship, spirituality, law, art, community service and local custom.

Sunni and Shia traditions developed different histories of authority and leadership, alongside diverse legal schools, theological approaches and Sufi paths. Muslim societies have flourished across Africa, the Middle East, South Asia, Central Asia, Southeast Asia, Europe and the Americas. Islam should not be represented as one ethnicity, one political system or one uniform cultural practice.

Knowledge, law and historical exchange

Muslim scholars worked in mathematics, astronomy, medicine, geography, philosophy, optics, literature and law. Their work involved original research, commentary, translation and criticism. Knowledge moved through Arabic, Persian, Greek, Syriac, Sanskrit and other languages, with different centres contributing at different times. It is more accurate to describe these histories as networks of exchange than as a single transfer from one civilisation to another.
