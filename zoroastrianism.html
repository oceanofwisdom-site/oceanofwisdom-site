Zoroastrianism — Zarathustra and Ahura Mazda

Origins and historical setting

Zoroastrianism is an ancient religious tradition rooted in the Iranian world. Its prophet is known as Zarathustra or Zoroaster. The date of his life remains debated, and the tradition developed over many centuries in ancient Iran.

Central teachings

Ahura Mazda is revered as the Wise Lord. Zoroastrian ethics emphasise choosing truth over falsehood and constructive action over harm. Its well-known ideal is good thoughts, good words and good deeds.

Texts, practices and communities

The Avesta is the sacred textual collection; the Gathas are hymns traditionally associated with Zarathustra. Fire symbolises purity and serves as a focus of worship, rather than being worshipped as a deity itself.

Legacy and continuing life

Zoroastrian communities shaped Iranian religious and cultural history. Later diasporas include the Parsi community in India. Historical study distinguishes ancient evidence, later religious memory and modern scholarly debate.

Origins and the problem of dating

Zoroastrianism emerged in the Iranian cultural world, but the date of Zarathustra (Zoroaster) remains debated. The language of the Gathas is Old Avestan and is linguistically distinct from later Avestan texts. Linguistic, textual and comparative evidence can help scholars estimate historical settings, but it does not produce a universally accepted birth date or biography for the prophet.

The religion developed through many centuries and under different political conditions. Zoroastrian traditions were influential in several Iranian empires, including the Sasanian period, but it is misleading to imagine that beliefs and institutions remained unchanged across all regions and eras.

The Avesta and the Gathas

The Avesta is the sacred collection of texts in the Avestan language. It includes materials of different genres and periods, including liturgical, ritual and devotional compositions. The Gathas are a group of hymns traditionally associated with Zarathustra and occupy a distinctive place in Zoroastrian worship and interpretation.

The textual history is complex. Much of the material was transmitted orally before being written down in manuscripts, and the surviving collection is not identical to a complete original book from one moment in time. Later Middle Persian translations and commentaries, known as Zand, are important for understanding how communities interpreted the older language and teachings.

Ethics, truth and ritual

Ahura Mazda is revered as the Wise Lord. Zoroastrian ethical teaching gives central importance to aša—often rendered as truth, order or rightness—and its opposition to falsehood or disorder. The familiar phrase “good thoughts, good words, good deeds” summarises an ethical ideal, but it should not replace study of the more complex language of the texts.

Fire is treated as a sacred symbol of purity and is central to important rituals; it is not simply worshipped as a deity. Purity practices, prayers, seasonal festivals and community ceremonies differ across historical and contemporary Zoroastrian settings.

Communities across Iran and the diaspora

Zoroastrian communities continue in Iran and elsewhere, including the Parsi communities of India and diaspora communities around the world. Their histories include migration, changing legal and political circumstances, reform movements and debates about identity and religious belonging. A complete account should include living Zoroastrians, not only ancient imperial history.
