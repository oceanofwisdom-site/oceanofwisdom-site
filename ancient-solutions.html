Ancient Solutions to Modern Problems

What can historical societies teach us about water, heat, food, materials and living within ecological limits?

Across the Indian subcontinent, communities developed practical responses to demanding climates and landscapes. Their systems were never identical, and they were not universally sustainable. Yet carefully studied examples reveal design principles—capture seasonal water, work with terrain, shade buildings, maintain shared infrastructure—that can inform present-day choices when tested against modern science and local needs.

FIELD NOTE 01

Water in a dry landscape: Dholavira

Dholavira, a Harappan city on the arid island of Khadir in Gujarat, depended on two seasonal streams. Its archaeological remains include a series of reservoirs, water-harnessing and drainage features integrated with a carefully planned fortified settlement. UNESCO dates occupation broadly to about 3000–1500 BCE and describes a long sequence of growth, adaptation and decline. The lesson is not that one ancient design can simply be copied. It is that water planning was embedded in urban form: catchments, storage, streets, drainage, materials and settlement organisation worked as a connected system.

Evidence & limits: The evidence is archaeological: excavated reservoirs, channels, city layout and construction remains. The exact operating rules and seasonal volumes cannot be reconstructed in every detail. We should therefore distinguish visible infrastructure from hypotheses about its day-to-day management.
FIELD NOTE 02

Stepwells: water access, architecture and shade

Stepwells provided access to groundwater through descending flights of steps. Their form changed across regions and centuries. Rani-ki-Vav at Patan, built in the 11th century CE as a memorial, is an elaborate seven-level structure combining a well, tank, pavilions and sculptural programmes. UNESCO describes it as both a functional water structure and an inverted-temple-like work of architecture. A 2024 peer-reviewed study examines passive-cooling features in medieval stepwells, including orientation, shaded spaces, pavilions and openings to the sky.

Evidence & limits: A stepwell is not a universal climate-control device. Cooling performance varies with geometry, season, humidity, ventilation and water level. Modern use of these principles requires building-physics analysis, safety standards and local climate data.
FIELD NOTE 03

Tanks, catchments and community maintenance

Across many parts of South Asia, tanks, village ponds, channels and wells were adapted to monsoon patterns and local topography. A tank system can slow runoff, store water for later use and support irrigation, but only when its feeder channels, bunds, sluices and catchment remain functional. The social arrangements that organise desilting, repair, access and water sharing are as important as the earthwork itself.

Evidence & limits: Avoid treating all tanks as one ancient system or assuming every historic arrangement was equitable. Dates, ownership and access varied. A local case study should be grounded in inscriptions, archival records, archaeology and community histories wherever available.
FIELD NOTE 04

Climate-responsive buildings: shade, mass and air

Traditional buildings across hot regions used different combinations of orientation, courtyards, verandas, deep eaves, screened openings, thick walls and locally available materials. These features can reduce direct solar gain or moderate temperature swings, but effectiveness depends on climate. A strategy that works in a hot-dry region may be unsuitable in a hot-humid coastal setting. The strongest contemporary approach combines tested passive design with efficient mechanical systems when needed.

Evidence & limits: Historical buildings were shaped by labour, status, materials and access to resources. They should not be romanticised as automatically comfortable, low-carbon or suitable for every household. Compare measured performance, not just appearance.
FIELD NOTE 05

From historical principle to responsible modern adaptation

A useful adaptation process has five steps: understand the local climate and water balance; identify the historical principle; test it with engineering and public-health evidence; consult the people who will maintain and use it; monitor performance over time. For water, this may mean protecting catchments and restoring recharge pathways. For buildings, it may mean shade, cross-ventilation and appropriate thermal mass. For agriculture, it may mean matching crops and soil practices to local rainfall rather than imposing a single template.
